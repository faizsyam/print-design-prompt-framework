# Strategic Analysis & Brief Deconstruction

*Used in Phase 1 of `SKILL.md`. Every decision here follows the Decision Protocol in `SKILL.md`, Section 2, and Phase 1 findings are compiled into the Decision Context.*

This reference guide establishes the principles and analytical methods for deconstructing a business brief, physical media substrate, and operating context into actionable design constraints before synthesized visual direction occurs.

---

## 1. Business Brief Deconstruction Framework

Every print design brief contains structural variables that govern visual prioritization. Deconstruct the input brief across five core dimensions:

```
      ┌───────────────────────────┐        ┌───────────────────────────┐
      │    BUSINESS POSITIONING   │        │  COMMUNICATION OBJECTIVE  │
      │   Market Position & Value │        │ Outcome the Piece Drives  │
      └─────────────┬─────────────┘        └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                    │                                    │
                    └─────────────────┬──────────────────┘
                                      │
        ┌─────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┐
        │                             │                             │
┌───────┴─────────────────┐           │           ┌─────────────────┴───────┐
│    MEDIA & PLACEMENT    │           │           │    TARGET PSYCHOLOGY    │
│ Dimensions, Aspect Ratio│           │           │ Decision Speed, Trigger │
│ Material, Setting, Light│           │           │ Points, Trust Signals   │
└───────┬─────────────────┘           │           └─────────────────┬───────┘
        │                             │                             │
        └─────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┘
                                      │
                       ┌──────────────┴────────────────┐
                       │    CONTENT & COPY DENSITY     │
                       │  Hard Data, Hierarchy Levels  │
                       └───────────────────────────────┘
```

### 1. Business Domain, Market Positioning & Objective
* **Positioning:** Positioning is a spectrum across several axes (price accessibility, exclusivity, formality, audience, emotional register), not a fixed set of tiers; most brands sit between or across them. Resolve where the brand sits on each axis from the brief. The more price or value is the audience's decision driver, the more immediate price visibility and strong contrast are required. The more perceived quality, craft, or exclusivity is the driver, the more restraint, negative space, refined scale, subtle texture, and material authenticity carry the message. Intermediate positions blend these. Positioning is expressed through signals the setting can credibly carry (see Placement Context & Contextual Register), not through the visual register of a different setting.
* **Communication Objective:** Identify the single outcome the piece exists to produce in the reader, as implied by the brief. The objective, not the business category, decides which content is primary and whether conversion elements (price hooks, calls to action) belong in the hierarchy at all.
* **Business Background:** Its history, scale, maturity, ambitions, and relationship with its locale and audience. Background decides how much recognition already exists, how much credibility must still be earned, and what identity the business can authentically carry.
* **Intended Response:** What the reader should feel and do on encountering the piece. The felt response is the target the mood is designed backward from, and the action is the target the hierarchy serves.
* **Core Value Proposition (USP):** Isolate what differentiates the business from competitors and decide which layer will carry it. The USP is a message to allocate, not automatically the theme (`02`, Section 1).
* **Distinctive Substance:** Capture what is distinctive about the business, product, brand name, and audience (origin, craft, sensory qualities, meaning, identity). Read the brand name for all it contains (the meaning, persona, and associations of every part), including parts the brief does not emphasize: emphasis shows what the client wants said, not necessarily the richest material to build from. This is the raw material for the brand theme in Phase 2 (`02`, Section 1).
* **Message Inventory:** List the messages the piece must carry (what the brand is, what it offers, how it should feel, what the reader should do) and note which the hard content and any pictorial subject will already carry. What remains is the work of the identity layers (theme, mark, type, color, ornament).

### 2. Target Audience Psychology & Viewing Context
* **Reading Time & Decision Speed:** Reading time is continuous. It is set by how the reader encounters the piece: in motion or stationary, by choice or incidentally, and how much else competes for attention. The shorter the exposure, the fewer hierarchy levels and focal points the piece can carry (at the shortest exposures: a single focal point and roughly three levels or fewer, high contrast, large display scale). Longer exposure allows structured blocks, secondary features, sub-branding, fine typography, and detailed information.
* **Environmental Noise & Viewing Distance:** Distance is also continuous and differs per element (see Physical Scale below). The farther an element is read from, the more its legibility depends on size, value contrast, and shape clarity; how these are achieved remains a stylistic decision. The closer it is read from, the more tactile textures, delicate detail, secondary copy, and subtle pattern become effective.

### 3. Gaps in the Brief
When the brief leaves a parameter unstated or ambiguous (dimensions, setting, audience, objective, or any other), infer the most plausible value from the remaining parameters and the placement context, and proceed. An inferred parameter never adds or alters hard content.

### 4. Context Synthesis: Weights & Tensions
Deconstruction yields parameters; synthesis yields the design problem. Complete it before any foundational decision.
* **Weights:** Judge how much each parameter governs in this brief. The dominant parameters set the decisions; the others constrain them.
* **Constraints:** Separate fixed constraints (physics, substrate, supplied content) from flexible ones (preferences, conventions). The design finds its room in the flexible ones.
* **Tensions:** Name where serving one parameter strains another, across business, audience, objective, medium, and placement.
* **Use:** The foundational decisions (`SKILL.md`, Section 2) are the answers to these weights and tensions. A foundational decision that resolves no tension is likely the prototype.

## 2. Media Substrate & Physical Mechanics

Print designs exist in physical space. Physical constraints dictate spatial zoning, typographic scale ratios, and margin requirements.

### Canvas Shape & Visibility
* **Dominant Axis:** The long axis of the canvas sets the primary reading direction and the natural flow of zones. Near-equal axes call for a centered or radial organization instead of a directional flow.
* **Visibility Map:** Determine which regions of the canvas are reliably seen and which are compromised by mounting, folds, trim, motion, eye-level or ground-level obstruction, or surrounding structure. Concentrate high-tier weight in reliable regions and keep critical content out of compromised ones.
* **Commitment to the Axis:** The more extreme the ratio, the more the composition must commit to its axis, with elements, type, and weight following it.
* **Non-Standard Formats:** For any other shape or construction (unconventional ratios, multi-panel, folded, wraparound, non-rectangular, dimensional), treat each independently seen face as its own canvas and design the continuity between faces.

### Physical Scale & Legibility Floor
Aspect ratio describes shape; physical dimensions and viewing distance determine how large content must actually be.
* **Legibility Floor (a lower bound, never a target):** Each element has a minimum physical size below which it stops being readable at the distance from which it is actually read. Derive it from the physical dimensions and that distance, never from canvas proportion. Any size at or above the floor is functionally legible; where within that range an element sits is decided by hierarchy and balance.
* **Distance Belongs to the Tier:** Viewing distance does not apply equally to every element. The farthest distance sets the size demanded by Tier 1 only. Tier 2 is read while approaching. Tier 3 is read by a reader who has chosen to engage, at the distance that reader then adopts. Large media are often read at more than one distance, so each element is sized for the distance at which it is meant to be read.
* **Scale Relationship:** Compare the floor-sized content footprint to the canvas. On large media the floor is far below what the canvas can hold, so lower-tier elements can sit near their floors without losing function, and the headroom belongs to Tier 1 scale and negative space. On small media the floor binds, so every element must remain readable at its floor and content is constrained.

### Placement Context & Contextual Register
A piece lives in a place. The setting determines what looks native and what looks out of place, so read the setting before choosing any direction:
* **Setting:** The kind of place the business occupies, its formality, and what a visitor expects of it.
* **Host & Surroundings:** What the piece is mounted on or in front of (material, color, form, condition), the neighboring signage, architecture, and competing media, and the ambient light and the times it is viewed.
* **Audience State:** What the viewer is doing, how fast, and in what frame of mind when the piece is encountered, and the cultural and local codes through which they read it.
* **Production & Lifespan:** The print process, substrate, finish, weather exposure, expected durability, and whether the piece is permanent or temporary. The design must look right as produced on that substrate, not only as imagined.
* **Adjacent Touchpoints:** Other brand surfaces the piece sits beside or must align with.
* **Contextual Register:** From the above, derive the range of finish, formality, realism, and production character that looks native in this setting. Positioning, theme, and style are expressed inside that range. Perceived quality is carried by restraint, craft, clarity, and consistency, which work in any register, whereas cues imported from another setting read as out of place even when they look refined.
* **Belonging and Distinction:** The piece must belong to its setting and stand apart from its neighbors. Distinction comes from a clear differentiator within the setting's range (idea, color relationship, form, or voice), never from adopting a register the setting does not carry or from intensity alone.

## 3. Content Filtering & Structural Hierarchy

Brief inputs combine non-negotiable requirements with soft preferences. The designer AI must filter and categorize all contents before spatial organization.

### Content Categorization Protocol

1. **Hard Content (Mandatory & Inviolable):**
   * Brand Name / Wordmark
   * Specific Descriptor or Category Tag
   * Required Copy / Taglines
   * Listed Menu Items, Services, or Variants (including exact names and ingredients if provided)
   * Stated Prices or Price Hooks
   * Exact Contact Details (Phone, WhatsApp, Instagram, Physical Address)
   * Explicit Badges or Trust Claims
   * Any other copy or element the brief supplies or designates as required
   * *Rule:* Every piece of hard content provided in the brief MUST appear in the final prompt. Never drop, truncate, or reword hard copy; only its layout (line breaks, grouping) may be adapted for visual density.
   * *Rule:* Descriptive detail attached to a listed item (composition, ingredients, specifications) is hard content when the brief lists it among the required contents, and is visual reference only when the brief frames it that way. When ambiguous, treat it as hard content and tier it by its role.

2. **Soft Preferences (Directional Guidance):**
   * Suggested colors, mood descriptors, style ideas.
   * *Rule:* Evaluate soft preferences against design principles. Synthesize them if they reinforce brand goals; adjust or refine them if they create visual conflict or compromise print legibility.

3. **Content Density Management:**
   * High copy volume in constrained spaces requires grouping related data (see `03`, Section 3) rather than shrinking text below its legibility floor.
   * Space is allocated to required content at its legibility floor first. Anything beyond content (imagery, ornament) receives only the space that remains.

4. **Attention Tiering (Reading Priority):**
   * After categorizing, rank every content element by *when the reader needs it*, not by what type of element it is:
     * **Tier 1 — Glance:** What must register first, at full viewing distance within the available reading time, for the piece to achieve its objective. The element that fills this role is determined, never assumed; the brand identity is Tier 1 only when establishing recognition is what the piece must do.
     * **Tier 2 — Scan:** Supporting content that builds interest while the reader approaches.
     * **Tier 3 — Deliberate:** Reference details needed only after the reader has decided to engage or act.
   * *Rule (Focus Determination):* Decide the primary focus from the objective and the reader's task; the reader's mode of engagement and duration with the medium; the structure of the content (whether the content itself is the offering the reader must scan) and the resulting reception balance (item 5); how much recognition the setting and the audience's familiarity with the brand already supply (the more is supplied, the more identity can recede); and the weight distribution native to the chosen style direction. Set the tiering here and confirm it in Phase 3, once the theme, style, and pictorial decisions are known. The element that fills Tier 1 is the primary focus and forms the focal anchor of the composition.
   * *Rule:* Tier is decided by an element's role in this brief's decision path, not by its category. Visual weight is a budget allocated in descending order by tier; a lower tier never out-weighs a higher one, and raising one element's weight is paid for by others. Weight is carried by several independent dimensions (scale, area, value contrast, hue and chroma distinctiveness, placement on the reading path, isolation by surrounding space, and detail density). Hierarchy must hold across them together: an element that is small but shares a higher tier's color, or occupies a prime position, still competes.
   * *Rule:* Purely ornamental elements carry no content tier. They rank below all content in weight and never compete with any tier.
   * *Rule:* Tier 3 content must be legible, not noticeable. It is never boosted for recognition at a glance.
   * *Rule:* The reading time from Section 1 limits how many Tier 2 elements can be carried; surplus Tier 2 content is grouped or demoted.

5. **Pictorial Commitment (Optional, Graded Visual Subject):**
   * A pictorial subject (product imagery, illustration, photography, or any depicted subject) is optional and never assumed. A piece can be carried entirely by typography, the mark, pattern, color, and composition.
   * *Rule:* Admit one only when depicting the subject does work the other layers cannot (recognition, desire, comprehension, or atmosphere) within the reading time, and when the content load leaves room for it after required content is placed at its legibility floor.
   * *Rule:* A pictorial subject is ranked like any content element, by its role in the decision path: it may be the Tier 1 anchor, a supporting Tier 2 element, ornament, or absent. The focal anchor is whichever Tier 1 element serves the objective, never the pictorial subject by default.
   * *Rule (Reception Balance):* Every piece sits between looked-at (recognition, desire, or atmosphere carries the objective) and read (the information itself is the offering). Judge the position from the objective, the reader's task, the medium's mode of use, reading time, and content load. The more the piece is read, the more canvas belongs to information and the less the pictorial subject can claim.
   * *Rule (Graded Commitment):* Pictorial presence is a gradient, not a switch: absent, ambient (cropped, partial, tonal, or background-level), supporting, or dominant. Choose the lowest grade that still does the job the other layers cannot. Dominance requires that the piece is looked-at, that the subject's job is the Tier 1 job, and that required content fits at its floor without taking the subject's area.
   * *Rule (Sufficiency):* The subject takes the smallest scale and weight at which it still does its job. A subject can hold Tier 1 without dominating area, because registering first is achieved by contrast, isolation, and placement as well as size; area is spent last (`03`, Section 5.1).
   * *Rule (Area Budget):* Canvas area goes first to required content at its floor, then to breathing room. What remains is a ceiling for the subject, never an entitlement, and surplus may go to type scale, negative space, or other layers instead. The more information the piece carries, the more the subject recedes in scale, then in visibility, down to absent.

## 4. Regional & Local Market Dynamics (UMKM Contexts)

When designing for local micro, small, and medium enterprises (UMKM) and vibrant street-level commercial environments (e.g., Southeast Asian / Indonesian market contexts), specific environmental factors shape design execution. Apply each only where it holds for the brief:

### 1. Street-Level Visual Competition
* **Visual Density:** Commercial streets, food centers, and traditional markets (*pasar*) present intense visual clutter (competing signs, overhead wires, varied lighting). 
* **Design Response:** The piece must separate itself from its surroundings through value contrast and clarity of structure. The means, and the degree of stylistic intensity, follow the brand's own identity (Phase 2); separation does not require boldness.

### 2. Value Communication & Trust Signals
* **Price Anchoring:** Where price is a decision driver for the local audience, price hooks operate as primary visual hooks rather than secondary details. Where it is not, they follow their tier.
* **Trust & Quality Signals:** Trust and quality claims supplied by the brief take the treatment their tier and the audience's need for credibility call for. Their weight follows their tier.
* **Direct Mobile Action:** WhatsApp handles and Instagram usernames serve as conversion channels that the reader uses after the hook has landed. Their recognition aids, size, and weight follow their tier: sized to the legibility floor and kept subordinate unless contact itself is the brief's primary focus.

### 3. Cultural Authenticity vs. Modern Polish
* **Preserving Heritage:** Traditional food, craft, or service businesses must communicate mastery and authenticity without appearing outdated or unhygienic.
* **Modern Refinement:** Combine heritage cues drawn from the brand's own heritage (applied as idea or structure, per `02`, Section 1) with contemporary structure and a sharp typographic hierarchy to project professional reliability.
